# N questions — country games

An exploratory game for Jason and Ramya about the countries that shaped
their English. Country of upbringing is the target. English-learning
environment is evidence, and may lead somewhere different.

## Shared rules

Each numbered question is a separate game. Its header tells you who
answers and how. Switch roles and repeat for the other person.

For a partner game, let the partner predict first, then let the speaker
confirm or correct. Describe actual speech you have heard. You can
recognize several forms or none of them. Add a natural alternative.

These are usage choices, including regional forms and learner patterns.
A familiar construction can be grammatical in one variety and unusual
in another. “Sounds like them” is the useful judgment.

Keep actual countries secret until the reveal. We will use text answers
and partner observations. This version focuses on country. It leaves
age, sex, pronunciation tests, and interactions between dimensions for
another design.

## The games

### 1. The late friend

**Rules — Partner predicts, speaker confirms. Casual question formation.**

Your partner is texting a friend who is late. Which message sounds most
like something they would actually write?

A. Where are you?
B. Where you are?
C. Where you at?
D. Where you?
E. Another wording.

Which have you actually seen them use? Could texting shorthand explain
the choice? The speaker now gives their own usual message.

### 2. How long?

**Rules — Partner predicts, speaker confirms. Tense and aspect.**

Your partner moved into a place five years ago and still lives there.
Which sounds most familiar in their ordinary speech?

A. I've lived here for five years.
B. I'm living here for five years.
C. I live here since five years.
D. I moved here five years ago.
E. Another wording.

Separate what they say from what they were taught to write.

### 3. The explanation clicks

**Rules — Partner predicts, speaker confirms. Progressive aspect.**

Someone has just made an instruction clear. Which is your partner most
likely to say?

A. Now I understand.
B. Now I'm understanding.
C. I get it now.
D. Now I got it.
E. Another wording.

If several fit, describe the different situations.

### 4. Checking the plan

**Rules — Partner predicts, speaker confirms. Question tags.**

Which ending have you heard your partner use after this statement?

“You're coming tomorrow, ...”

A. aren't you?
B. right?
C. isn't it?
D. no?
E. eh?
F. Another ending, or no ending.

Give an actual example if you remember one.

### 5. A specific day

**Rules — Partner predicts, speaker confirms. Focus placement.**

Your partner wants to emphasize that the meeting is today specifically.
Which sounds most familiar?

A. The meeting is today.
B. The meeting is today itself.
C. Today only we have the meeting.
D. It's today that we have the meeting.
E. Another wording.

Explain what their version means. Do not assume these alternatives
express exactly the same meaning.

### 6. The document request

**Rules — Partner predicts, speaker confirms. Request wording.**

Your partner asks a colleague for a document due today. Which sounds
most like them?

A. Could you send me the document today?
B. Please send me the document today.
C. Kindly send the document today.
D. Can you send across the document today?
E. Another wording.

Which words would change for a friend, boss, or unfamiliar colleague?

### 7. One piece of information

**Rules — Partner predicts, speaker confirms. Count nouns.**

Which expression sounds familiar from your partner's casual speech?

A. I need some advice.
B. I need an advice.
C. I need some advices.
D. Can you advise me?
E. Another wording.

Then try information versus informations. Record the two judgments
separately but treat them as potentially related evidence.

### 8. Finding an object

**Rules — Partner predicts, speaker confirms. Embedded questions.**

Which question sounds most like your partner?

A. Do you know where the charger is?
B. Do you know where is the charger?
C. You know where the charger is?
D. Where's the charger?
E. Another wording.

If they avoid the longer form, say so rather than inventing an answer.

### 9. Finished yet?

**Rules — Partner predicts, speaker confirms. Completion.**

A meal is still in progress. Which reply sounds most familiar?

A. I haven't finished yet.
B. I didn't finish yet.
C. I'm not finished yet.
D. I still haven't eaten finish.
E. Another wording.

Choose forms actually heard. The speaker can reject every offered form.

### 10. A familiar article pattern

**Rules — Partner predicts, speaker confirms. Article choices.**

Which differences have you actually noticed in your partner's speech?

A. I'm going to the office / I'm going to office.
B. She's in the hospital / She's in hospital.
C. He's a teacher / He's teacher.
D. The life is complicated / Life is complicated.
E. None of these, or another example.

Judge each pair separately. Hospital usage has established regional
variation. The other pairs may involve context, register, or learning.

### 11. Two actions, one person

**Rules — Partner predicts, speaker confirms. Agreement.**

Which sentence have you heard your partner naturally use about a third
person?

A. She doesn't know.
B. She don't know.
C. She not know.
D. She doesn't knows.
E. Another wording.

Only report a pattern you recognize. An isolated slip is weak evidence.

### 12. Make the request naturally

**Rules — Speaker answers first, partner compares. Free production.**

Ask a colleague to send you a document today. Say one sentence before
looking back at the earlier options.

The partner then names any difference between the prediction and this
answer. If reading the previous game influenced you, say so.

### 13. A bundle of everyday nouns

**Rules — Speaker answers directly. Vocabulary.**

Give your usual word for each item.

- A small portable electric light.
- A machine carrying people between floors.
- The walking area beside a road.
- People standing in order waiting to pay.
- The toilet room in a home.

The partner can add observed alternatives afterward. Say which words
came from school or media if you know.

### 14. Everyday verbs

**Rules — Speaker answers directly. Verb choice.**

Complete each naturally.

- Before bed, I ___ the light.
- To reach that person by phone, I'll ___ them.
- At the station, we ___ the train.
- At the hotel, I'll ___ a room for next week.

Offer your own phrasing. Context can permit several perfectly ordinary
answers, so the combination is more informative than a single verb.

### 15. Everyday adjectives

**Rules — Speaker answers directly. Adjective choice.**

What word would you use for each situation?

- A meal tastes very good.
- A task requires little effort.
- A person is annoying you.
- Something costs much less than expected.

Give your first natural word, then a sentence using it. Say if it is a
borrowed word or an expression you mainly learned online.

### 16. A large number

**Rules — Speaker answers directly. Number naming.**

Say 1,250,000 in ordinary English. Then write it with the digit grouping
you would use for someone back home.

How would you shorten it in a casual message? A convention learned
through work or school may differ from the country you grew up in.

### 17. A familiar small scene

**Rules — Speaker narrates, partner notices. Connected sample.**

In three sentences, tell a friend that you arrived late, could not find
the entrance, and called someone for help.

The partner identifies one grammatical or vocabulary choice that sounds
especially familiar from this speaker. If none stands out, record none.

### 18. Falsify the leading guess

**Rules — Both answer, one speaker at a time. Follow-up selection.**

We will name the leading country hypotheses and the clues behind them.
Which observed phrasing would be surprising under the leading guess?

Choose one follow-up that distinguishes the remaining hypotheses.
Check a linguistic source before claiming a country-specific pattern.
Keep a mixed-learning-history explanation available.

### 19. The reveal

**Rules — Speaker answers directly. Check the result.**

Which country or countries did you grow up in? Where did you mostly
learn English? Did family, schooling, migration, work, or media shape
different parts of your English?

Compare the answer with the earlier guess. Record both matches and
mistakes, including any country we failed to consider.

## Choosing the next game

Begin with a partner grammar prediction, then a vocabulary game.
Alternate so one answer style does not dominate.

Keep a separate hypothesis trail for each person. A partner's claim
that a phrase is common in a country is an observation to investigate,
rather than an established country label. Their direct familiarity
with the speaker can be useful even when that broader claim is wrong.

Choose the next game that best separates the current country hypotheses.
Use broad coverage initially and more specific, source-checked probes
later. A shared educational tradition can span many countries.

Repeated evidence from one pattern should count once. Advice and
information choices may share a grammatical cause. Several vocabulary
choices may share one schooling influence. Conflicts invite follow-up.

No numerical confidence or optimality claim is justified without a
calibrated answer dataset. A list of alternatives is a probe, not a
validated classification rule. Many countries will remain indistinguishable
from these text answers. Say so when that happens.

## Background sources

- [eWAVE](https://ewave-atlas.org/introduction) supplies a framework for
  comparing grammatical variation across worldwide English varieties.
- [International Dialects of English Archive](https://www.dialectsarchive.com/)
  documents worldwide variation and speaker backgrounds.

This game is an original exploratory design. These sources do not
validate the specific prompts or establish their country-level accuracy.
