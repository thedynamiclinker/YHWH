# N questions

An adaptive game about where your English comes from.

We are trying to identify the places and speech communities that shaped
your English. Those may differ from where you were born or grew up.
This is an exploratory question bank, with worldwide scope. Its accuracy
and information efficiency have not been measured.

## How we will play

- One question at a time, shown in writing as well as asked aloud.
- Start each answer with your name so the two answer trails stay separate.
- Answer for casual speech with friends. School grammar may differ.
- Either person can add what the other actually says, after their answer.
- Mixed, neither, unfamiliar, and depends are useful answers.
- Give your natural answer before reading the suggested alternatives.
- Keep birthplace and home languages secret until the final reveal.
- We will compare multiple clues before making a tentative regional guess.
- We can stop when the clues become weak or you want to reveal the answer.

## Opening questions

### 1. A late friend

A friend was supposed to arrive ten minutes ago. Send them a casual
one-sentence message asking where they are.

Write what you would actually send. Then, if it is different, write what
people around you growing up would have said in English.

### 2. Learning English

By about age ten, where did you mostly hear English?

- At home.
- With friends or in the neighborhood.
- In school lessons.
- Through television, films, games, or the internet.
- Several of these.
- I learned it mainly later.

You can describe the mix without naming languages or places yet.

### 3. Talking to a group

How do you naturally address several friends at once?

For example, finish this message in your own words.

“Are ___ coming with us?”

After answering, compare with you, you all, y'all, you guys, you lot,
youse, you people, and any expression we missed.

### 4. Everyday word bundle

What do you call each of these in everyday English?

- The moving stairs in a shopping center.
- The machine that carries people between floors.
- The place beside a road where pedestrians walk.
- The room at home containing a toilet.
- A small portable electric light.
- The line of people waiting to pay.

Use familiar words. Say if you learned one mainly from media or school.

### 5. A short story

In three or four sentences, tell us about getting somewhere late.
A made-up ordinary incident is fine.

Use the English you use with friends. A spontaneous sample helps us
choose which follow-up would actually distinguish the remaining options.

## Follow-up question bank

Choose relevant questions from this bank. We will skip redundant ones.

### 6. An unfinished meal

You are still eating. Someone asks whether you want dessert.
What would you say?

After answering, compare “I haven't finished yet,” “I'm not finished yet,”
“I didn't finish yet,” and your own version.

### 7. Since when?

You started living in a place in 2020 and still live there.
Tell a friend that fact in one sentence.

After answering, compare “I've lived here since 2020,” “I'm living here
since 2020,” and any version you actually use.

### 8. Understanding

Someone explains a confusing instruction, and now it makes sense.
What do you say?

After answering, compare “Now I understand,” “Now I'm understanding,”
“I get it now,” and your own expression.

### 9. A polite request

Ask a colleague to send you a document today. Make the request sound
normal for you, rather than maximally formal.

Would you naturally add words such as please, kindly, just, only,
can, could, or would? Which ones, and where?

### 10. Confirming a plan

Finish this casual question.

“You're coming tomorrow, ___?”

Would you use aren't you, right, isn't it, no, eh, another ending,
or simply a change in tone?

### 11. Agreement

A friend says, “That meeting was exhausting.” You strongly agree.
What short response comes naturally?

### 12. Emphasis

You want to explain that you mean today specifically.
How would you say it?

After answering, compare “I meant today,” “I meant today itself,”
“I only meant today,” and “Today, that's what I meant.”
These can have different meanings. Explain yours.

### 13. Articles in casual speech

Complete these as you would in a quick message.

“I need ___ advice.”
“She works at ___ hospital.”
“He's gone to ___ hospital.”
“We met at ___ university.”

Which choices change when you are writing formally? If hospital and
university feel different depending on the person's role, explain that.

### 14. A car problem

Your car will not start. Call a mechanic and describe the problem in
two sentences.

Follow up only if useful with bonnet/hood, boot/trunk, petrol/gas,
and indicator/turn signal. Familiarity with both is a valid answer.

### 15. A small purchase

Ask a shop worker for a plastic bag and ask how much it costs.
Use the phrasing you would normally use.

### 16. Family words

In your everyday English, what do you call your mother's sister,
your father's older brother, and a cousin?

Do the words change depending on age, respect, or whether you are
speaking directly to the person?

### 17. Addressing unfamiliar adults

What would you call an unfamiliar adult in a shop, or a friend's parent?
Would you use a first name, a title, auntie/uncle, sir/ma'am, or something
else? Describe the setting, because this varies within communities.

### 18. Time expressions

What does “half eight” mean to you, if anything?
How would you normally say 8:30 and 8:45?

### 19. Number grouping

How would you say 1,250,000 aloud in ordinary English?
How would you group its digits when writing it for someone back home?

### 20. A familiar local expression

What English expression did people around you use that outsiders
sometimes misunderstood?

Explain its meaning without naming the place yet.

### 21. School vocabulary

What did you call the final year before university, a mid-day school
meal, and the break between lessons?

If your schooling was in another language, tell us whether these are
translations or terms from an English-speaking school environment.

### 22. Food vocabulary

What do chips, biscuits, and tea mean in your everyday conversation?
For tea, does it ever name a meal? Context and multiple meanings count.

### 23. Direction and location

A friend cannot find your home. Give two sentences of directions from
an imaginary nearby shop. Use your natural English.

This gives us another sample without forcing a particular construction.

## Pronunciation branch

Use this only with a listener who can hear you, or with your own
judgments about sounds. A text transcript cannot preserve an accent.

### 24. Sound comparisons

For each pair, do the words rhyme, sound identical, or sound different
in your own speech?

- cot / caught
- wine / whine
- pool / pull
- full / fool
- ship / sheep

No pair identifies a place by itself. Skip any pair that feels unclear.

### 25. The R sound

Say car, hard, and car engine. Do you pronounce an R in each?
Does it change with the next word or with how carefully you speak?

### 26. Stress and familiar words

Say photograph, photography, record as a noun, and record as a verb.
Then say schedule, tomato, and advertisement.

A listener can describe what they hear. A transcript spelling the word
normally supplies no evidence about how it sounded.

## Optional reveal and refinement

### 27. Language background

What languages did you speak at home before age ten? Which language
was used at school? Include regional names if you know them.

This is voluntary direct information. Once supplied, it changes the game
from a blind dialect guess into an investigation of language history.

### 28. Childhood geography

Where did you spend ages five through fifteen? List moves and roughly
how long you spent in each place.

Compare this with our earlier guesses. Record misses as well as hits.

### 29. Explaining the mismatch

Which parts of your current English came from childhood, later moves,
work, a partner, school instruction, or media?

### 30. One local follow-up

After the reveal, choose a documented local vocabulary or pronunciation
contrast that might distinguish the actual regions still in question.
Check a source first. Do not invent a city-specific diagnostic.

## Notes for choosing the next question

The aim is to eliminate plausible explanations, using answers that add
new evidence.

1. Start with question 1, then 2 and 3. Use a natural sample early.
2. Keep separate records for Jason and Ramya.
3. Track several candidate English-learning environments, including mixed
   histories and an unknown option.
4. Choose the next question where those candidates predict different
   answers. Prefer natural production before multiple-choice prompts.
5. Avoid counting a cluster of school-taught vocabulary as several
   independent clues. These answers may share one cause.
6. Use a conflicting answer to revise candidates or ask about context.
7. Give qualitative confidence. Numerical probabilities need calibration.
8. Narrow to a city only when specific evidence supports that precision.
9. Stop when available questions cannot discriminate reliably.
10. Before the reveal, summarize the clues and the remaining alternatives.

A formal information-gain calculation would require probabilities for
answers given candidate backgrounds and a prior over those backgrounds.
We do not have that dataset here. Twenty binary answers distinguish
2^20 possibilities only under ideal conditions, including sufficiently
informative and reliable answers.

Article choices alone cannot establish a first language. Similar patterns
can arise through different learning histories, informal register,
regional English, or the immediate situation.

## Sources and scope

The question bank is an original exploratory design. The sources below
provide comparative linguistic context, rather than validation of this
particular questionnaire.

- [eWAVE introduction](https://ewave-atlas.org/introduction) describes
  expert judgments across worldwide English varieties, pidgins, and
  creoles, using 235 grammatical features.
- [International Dialects of English Archive](https://www.dialectsarchive.com/)
  provides recordings of English accents and dialects worldwide,
  including speakers of other first languages.
